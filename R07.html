Newer Doesn't Mean Trouble-Free

There's a persistent myth that new homes don't have plumbing problems. The reality is that production builders work to a budget, and plumbing fixtures are one of the easiest places to save money invisibly. The faucet that looks identical to the showroom model has a plastic cartridge instead of ceramic. The water heater is the minimum code-legal size with the thinnest tank walls. Supply lines to toilets and sinks are cheap vinyl rather than braided steel. None of this fails in year one — that's the point; it all survives the builder's one-year warranty and then starts failing in years five through twelve. We see the pattern so clearly we can almost date a North Redmond home by what's breaking. The good news: the pipes in the walls (usually PEX or copper) are fine. It's everything attached to them that needs attention.

The Builder-Grade Pattern We See

Let us walk you through the greatest hits. Plastic toilet and faucet supply lines are the scariest — they become brittle with age and burst without warning, and a burst supply line can dump gallons per minute into your home while you're at work. We replace these on sight with braided steel lines; it's cheap insurance. Low-end garbage disposals seize up or leak from the housing. Basic water heaters hit their 8-to-10-year wall right on schedule, often with no anode rod maintenance ever performed. And original shutoff valves — the cheap multi-turn kind — freeze open, so when you actually need to stop water to a fixture, you can't. Each of these is a small job individually. Together, they're the reason we recommend a whole-house plumbing audit around year ten: one visit, one list, prioritized by risk, instead of a surprise every six months.

Smart Upgrades for Newer Homes

You don't need to gut anything — strategic upgrades transform a builder-grade system. Start with braided steel supply lines and quarter-turn ball shutoffs at every fixture; this single afternoon of work eliminates the two most common flood sources in newer homes. As faucets fail, replace them with mid-range models with ceramic disc cartridges — you'll feel the difference daily and won't revisit them for decades. Have your water heater flushed and its anode rod checked; a $200 maintenance visit can add five years to a tank's life. Check your water pressure with a $10 gauge from the hardware store — North Redmond's municipal pressure runs high in spots, and anything over 80 PSI voids fixture warranties and shortens appliance life. A pressure-reducing valve adjustment (or installation) is quick and protects everything downstream. These aren't luxury upgrades; they're the corrections that bring a builder-grade home up to the standard it should have been built to.

Warranty vs. Reality

If your home is still within the builder's warranty period, document everything now. Builders cover defects, but their definition of "defect" narrows remarkably after closing — and "the faucet drips" gets classified as wear and tear faster than you'd think. Take photos of any plumbing issue, keep a dated log, and submit claims in writing before the warranty expires. What warranties almost never cover: water heater maintenance (your responsibility from day one), damage from frozen hose bibbs, and anything caused by "homeowner neglect" — a category that expands to fill available space. Once the warranty lapses, you're on your own, which is exactly when the failure wave arrives. Our advice to North Redmond homeowners: use the last six months of warranty to get a professional inspection, push every legitimate claim, and walk into year eleven with a prioritized upgrade plan instead of a surprise.
